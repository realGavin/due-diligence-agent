- Cash generation is strong and capital-light: $102.7B of operating cash flow and $96.7B of free cash flow (44.8% FCF margin) against only $6.0B of capex in FY2026, up from $1.8B in FY2023. <sub>[F24] [F52] [F53] [F28] [F25]</sub>
- The balance sheet is net-cash (-$2.1B net debt) with long-term debt flat at $8.5B against $10.6B of cash, and diluted shares actually declined from 25.07B to 24.51B, so growth has not required dilution or leverage. <sub>[F54] [F36] [F32] [F41] [F44] [F55]</sub>
- Technical entrenchment is evidenced by over $76.7B of cumulative R&D, support for 6,000 applications, and NVIDIA compute powering over 78% of the TOP500 supercomputer list, reinforcing switching costs beyond raw chip performance. <sub>[S5] [S8]</sub>

## Bear case (after red-team review)

- The China market is effectively closed, not just temporarily disrupted: after a $4.5B charge for H20 excess inventory, licenses granted in August 2025 generated only ~$60 million in H20 revenue, and a newer H200 licensing path granted in February 2026 has produced zero revenue to date while carrying a 25% import tariff. <sub>[S19] [S38] [S20] [S39]</sub>
- NVIDIA is increasingly financing its own demand ecosystem: $17.5B invested in illiquid, non-marketable private companies and infrastructure funds (including AI model makers that buy NVIDIA products) plus $3.5B in land, power, and shell guarantees to early-stage companies that 'may not become profitable in the near term, or at all,' creating circular-financing and counterparty risk. <sub>[S22] [S41] [S23] [S42]</sub>
- Gross margin quality shows underlying deterioration, not just a one-time hit: the net unfavorable impact of inventory provisions on gross margin rose to 2.6% in FY2026 from 2.3% in FY2025, and management attributes part of the FY2026 gross margin decrease to the Hopper-to-Blackwell transition itself, implying recurring execution risk with each new architecture cadence (Blackwell Ultra, Rubin). <sub>[S30] [S45] [S18]</sub>
- Growth is now dependent on factors outside NVIDIA's control: management states that customer/partner data center, energy, and capital availability is 'crucial' to future revenue and that energy capacity expansion is a 'complex, multi-year process,' while Data Center revenue (up 68% YoY) drives the bulk of total 65% growth, creating single-segment bottleneck exposure. <sub>[S17] [S36] [S25]</sub>
- Supply chain concentration remains a live risk despite diversification efforts into the U.S. and Latin America: manufacturing is still 'mainly concentrated in Asia,' and lead times have 'in some cases extended beyond twelve months,' challenging the assumption that current delivery cadence and margins are durable and scalable without disruption. <sub>[S21] [S40] [S30]</sub>
- The headline 100.0% three-year revenue CAGR (from $27.0B in FY2023 to $215.9B in FY2026) reflects a small starting base and coincides with the same period as the largest disclosed one-time disruption (the $4.5B H20 charge) and rising circular-financing exposure ($17.5B, $3.5B), so the historical rate alone does not demonstrate the growth trajectory can persist. <sub>[F1] [F4] [F51] [S19] [S22] [S23]</sub>

**Q: What is the realistic multi-year revenue potential from China given the H20 ($60M realized post-$4.5B charge) and H200 (25% tariff, zero revenue to date) licensing regimes, and could further U.S. or Chinese policy changes close this market entirely?**

- both figures appear[ing] side by side in the same Form 10-K for fiscal year ending January 2026 <sub>[W21]</sub>
- in February 2026, the USG granted a license that would allow Nvidia to ship small amounts of H200 products to specific China-based customers, but to date it has not generated any revenue under the H200 licensing program, and does not yet know whether any imports will be allowed into China <sub>[W22]</sub>
- "largely conceded" China's artificial intelligence chip market to Huawei <sub>[W27]</sub>
- "expect nothing" regarding approvals to sell advanced chips into the country <sub>[W28]</sub>
- China's internet regulator, the Cyberspace Administration of China, banned domestic tech companies from buying Nvidia AI chips <sub>[W30]</sub>
- Beijing had previously discouraged companies from buying these chips in late August, instead promoting alternatives from local manufacturers <sub>[W31]</sub>
- the export controls applicable to China are complex, addressing processing performance, performance density, interconnect bandwidth and memory bandwidth, and under the current rules and geopolitical landscape, Nvidia is unable to create and deliver a competitive product for China's data center market that receives approval from both the USG and the Chinese government <sub>[W32]</sub>

**Q: Is the rise in inventory-provision drag on gross margin (2.6% in FY2026 vs 2.3% in FY2025) expected to recur or worsen with the Blackwell Ultra/Rubin transitions, or is management guiding toward normalization?**

- gross margins guided between 74.9% and 75.0% indicate management expects the Rubin transition, still in early phases, will not create the type of cost headwinds that sometimes accompany major chip architecture transitions <sub>[W8]</sub>
- gross margin guidance was set at 74.0% (±50 bps), a slight step down from 75%, reflecting the higher mechanical and thermal complexity of scaling liquid-cooled, full-rack systems <sub>[W9]</sub>

**Q: What share of FY2026 Data Center revenue is concentrated among a small number of hyperscaler customers, and how sensitive is the growth outlook to any single customer's capex pullback given the disclosed energy/capital bottleneck?**

- two direct customers represented 22% and 14% of total annual revenue <sub>[W10]</sub>
- three direct customers represented 21%, 17%, and 16% of total revenue, all of which was primarily attributable to the Compute & Networking segment <sub>[W11]</sub>
- remained at approximately 50% of Data Center revenue, while the remaining 50% came from a continued diversification of customers, including AI Clouds, industrial, enterprise, and sovereign customers <sub>[W12]</sub>
- $193.7 billion, up 68% year on year <sub>[W14] [W15]</sub>
- Every data center is power constrained, with customers making critical architectural decisions based on performance per watt given these constraints and the need to maximize AI factory revenue <sub>[W16]</sub>
- $800 billion in 2026 and $1.3 trillion in 2027 <sub>[W17]</sub>
- non-hyperscaler growth...will represent roughly half of our data center business <sub>[W18]</sub>
- if a handful of major buyers slow capital expenditure, extend server replacement cycles or shift workloads toward internal accelerators, NVIDIA can feel the effect quickly <sub>[W19]</sub>

**Q: What specific steps and timeline exist for U.S./Latin America manufacturing capacity to reduce the current Asia concentration and the 12+ month lead times, and what capacity levels are targeted by which dates?**

- the new TSMC factory in Phoenix, Arizona is manufacturing NVIDIA Blackwell wafers in volume, meaning more advanced chips made in America and more systems assembled in America. <sub>[W33]</sub>
- has completed construction and is expected to commence production ahead of its revised 2028 schedule due to strong customer demand <sub>[W35]</sub>
- TSMC will begin installing equipment in the third quarter of 2026, paving the way to begin 3-nanometer chip production by 2027 <sub>[W36]</sub>
- began groundwork in April 2025, with production targeted by the end of the decade, possibly as early as 2027. <sub>[W37]</sub>
- approximately 30% of its 2nm and more advanced capacity will be located in Arizona once these facilities are completed <sub>[W38]</sub>
- TSMC sends 100% of chips to Taiwan to be packaged, even those made at its advanced chip fabrication plant in Phoenix, Arizona. <sub>[W39]</sub>
- TSMC is building its first U.S. advanced packaging facilities in Arizona this year, and ramping two new sites in Taiwan, <sub>[W40]</sub>
- TSMC didn't disclose a timeline for completion of the U.S. packaging sites. <sub>[W41]</sub>
- the cause of shortages is packaging and memory, not silicon — TSMC CoWoS throughput and HBM output cap NVIDIA shipments, not GPU die yield <sub>[W42]</sub>
- it has previously experienced and may continue to experience extended lead times of more than 12 months <sub>[W44]</sub>
- to secure future supply and capacity, it has paid premiums, provided deposits, and entered into long-term supply agreements and capacity commitments <sub>[W45]</sub>
- the plan projects that the first data center facilities will be operational during the second half of 2026 <sub>[W46]</sub>
